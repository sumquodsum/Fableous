Four bells, rung from the lightest down to the heaviest: 1 2 3 4. That order is called rounds. A bell on its wheel is too heavy to hurry, so between one row and the next it can trade places with a neighbour and no more. Ring every order the four can stand in, each once and none twice, and end in rounds again. That is the extent.

The Hundred and Sixty-Two

Every extent on four bells is one of these loops, begun at one row or another, rung one way round or the other, mirrored or not. Each is drawn on the solid from the same side. Those you ring by hand are inked in.

 

Of the Art

An English tower bell is hung on a wheel and rung full circle: mouth up, right round, mouth up again, once for every pull of the rope. It usually outweighs the person ringing it, sometimes by a ton or more, and it comes round in its own time, about once every two seconds. You cannot play a tune on a thing like that. What a ringer can do is hold the bell on the balance a moment longer, or let it turn a moment sooner, and so strike one place later or one place earlier than last time. One place, never two.

So from each row to the next, every bell either keeps its place or trades with a neighbour. Around 1600, English ringers began to build a music out of that single rule, a music with no tune in it at all, only the order of the bells, changing.

Two more rules came with it. A row once rung is not rung again: ringing that repeats itself is false, and ringing that never does is true. And the ringing ends where it began, in rounds. Take both rules as far as they go and you reach the extent: every order the bells can stand in, each rung once, and home.

On four bells that is twenty-four rows and about half a minute. On seven it is 5,040 rows and about three hours; the first such ringing known to have been true was at St Peter Mancroft in Norwich on the 2nd of May 1715, and took three hours and eighteen minutes. On eight bells it is 40,320 rows. Eight ringers rang that at the bell foundry in Loughborough on the 27th of July 1963, in two minutes under eighteen hours.

Nobody handed the ringers these. An extent is a single path through every row that never meets itself, and it has to be found. The first book on how to find them, Tintinnalogia, was printed in London in 1668, more than a century and a half before mathematics had the word group for what its author was handling.

The solid

Ask of each bell which place it is ringing in, and every row becomes a point in space. The twenty-four points are the corners of a solid with six square faces and eight six-sided ones. Two rows that differ by a single swap are joined by an edge. The commonest change in ringing, the cross, where both pairs swap at once, is not an edge. It runs corner to corner over a square face, and each square carries two of them. An extent is one closed loop on this solid that passes through every corner once.

The catalogue

There are 10,792 such loops that begin from rounds. Here they are numbered in dictionary order, with the changes ranked ×, 14, 12, 34, and it turns out that No. 1 is Plain Bob, the first method a ringer learns. Count two loops as one when they differ only in where they start, which way round they go, or by being mirrored front to back, and 162 remain. They are all drawn above.

Eleven have names any ringer would know, each ending in Minimus, the ringers’ word for four bells. In each of them the treble hunts plainly out to the back and home again while the other three bells work around it, the same eight changes three times over. Nearly all the rest have never been called anything. By custom, the first band to ring a method names it.

The bells

Nothing here is a recording. Each bell is added up from the partials a founder tunes: the hum, the prime, the tierce a minor third above it that makes a bell sound like a bell, the quint, the nominal. After every second row the band leaves one beat of silence, as English ringers do. It is why the ringing seems to walk.
